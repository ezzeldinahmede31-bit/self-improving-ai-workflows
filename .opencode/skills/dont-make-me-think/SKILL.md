Don't Make Me Think is the shortest path to usable web design: the goal is a page so self-evident the user does not pause to wonder. This skill applies Krug's rules to pages, dashboards, and any interface.

## The first rule
- A page should be self-evident: the user should be able to say 'of course' the moment they see it. If you need a manual or a tutorial, the design lost.
- There is no such thing as a user who reads the manual. People scan, skim, and click the first plausible thing.

## How users really behave
- They do not read; they scan. Design for scanning: clear headings, short paragraphs, and a strong visual hierarchy.
- They satisfice: they pick the first reasonable option instead of the best one. Make the right option the obvious first option.
- They muddle through: they will use a workaround rather than learn your system. Give them the easy path; do not force the correct model on them.

## The trunk test
- A new visitor should be able to identify what the site is, what it offers, and how to use it within five seconds of looking at it. If they cannot, the page failed its own identity test.

## Designing for skimming
- Use a clear visual hierarchy: size, color, and position make the important things stand out.
- Exploit conventions: users bring expectations from every other site. Reuse the familiar patterns (logo top-left, navigation where expected) instead of inventing new ones.
- Break pages into clearly defined areas so the eye knows where to look.
- Make the clickable obvious: buttons should look like buttons, links should look like links.
- Minimize noise: every extra element competes for attention. Cut what does not help.

## The home page lesson
- The home page is where you set expectations and guide the user. Ask: who is this for, what is here, where do I start? Answer all three above the fold with zero jargon.

## Testing the cheap way
- Usability testing with three users catches most of the big problems, early and cheaply. Watch them try the real task, note where they pause, fix, retest.
- Every pause is a signal. Users will not tell you they are confused; they will just leave or click the wrong thing. Watch, do not ask.

## Applying to dashboards and automations
- An n8n workflow or dashboard is usable if its intended operator can read it without a briefing. Name nodes with verbs and business language, keep the flow linear, and make the next step obvious.
- For a dashboard, the trunk test applies: five seconds to understand what it tracks and where to click.

## Hard rules
- Never make the user think about where to click, what a label means, or which step comes next; the design should answer it.
- Never force users to learn a proprietary model; use the conventions they already know.
- Never ship a page without a five-second identity test and a quick usability pass with a couple of real users.
