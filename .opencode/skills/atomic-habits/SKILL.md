Atomic Habits argues you do not rise to the level of your goals; you fall to the level of your systems. Tiny daily improvements compound, and identity drives behavior more than intention. This skill applies that to personal workflows, team practices, and automation design.

## Goals vs systems
- Goals give direction; systems give progress. Fix the system and the outcome follows; chase the goal and you fight the system every day.
- A 1% improvement each day compounds surprisingly fast; a 1% decline compounds down just as fast.

## The four laws of behavior change (and their inverses)
1. Make it obvious (inverse: make it invisible).
2. Make it attractive (inverse: make it unattractive).
3. Make it easy (inverse: make it difficult).
4. Make it satisfying (inverse: make it unsatisfying).
For every desired habit, run all four forward. For every bad habit, run all four backward.

## Identity-based habits
- Behavior is a mirror of identity. Instead of 'I want to run more', adopt 'I am a runner'. Instead of 'I want clean code', adopt 'I am a professional who does not ship broken work'.
- Every action is a vote for the identity you are building. A single lapse is one vote, not a sentence; just do not cast too many losing votes.

## Habit stacking
- Attach a new habit to an existing one: 'after I do X, I will do Y.' The old habit becomes the trigger for the new one.
- Start with the smallest reliable version: make the habit so small it cannot be skipped.

## The 2-minute rule
- Any habit can be reduced to a two-minute opening. 'Read' becomes 'open the book'. 'Write' becomes 'open the editor'. Once the opening is done, continuing is usually easy.
- The point is the identity repetition, not the two minutes.

## Environment design
- Arrange the environment so the good action is the path of least resistance: keep the tool on the desk, remove the distraction from the room.
- Design for one decision: default settings, templates, and prefilled flows make the right action the easy action.

## Applying to automation and delivery
- When a process keeps failing, redesign the system, not the willpower. Make the correct step the default, the easy step, the obvious step.
- A daily improvement loop (one small experiment per day, reviewed weekly) beats a big annual overhaul.
- Delete friction from the routine: fewer clicks, fewer approvals, fewer handoffs. Friction is the enemy of the system.

## Hard rules
- Never design a system that depends on motivation; motivation is unreliable. Make the good path easy and the bad path hard.
- Never track a goal without tracking the system that produces it; measure the action, and the outcome follows.
- Never judge a single lapse harshly; judge the system's design over many days.
