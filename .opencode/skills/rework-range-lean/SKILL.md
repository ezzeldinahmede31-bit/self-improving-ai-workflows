Rework argues the biggest threat to a product is not competitors but bloat: too many people, too many features, too much planning, and too much money. This skill applies that lean, ruthless-scope discipline to product work and automations.

## Core principles
- Small is not a stage. Staying small is a choice that keeps you nimble. Capacity that you never needed is overhead, not insurance.
- Constraints are a benefit. A tiny budget and a tiny team force focus. Assume the constraint, then design within it instead of complaining about it.
- Scratch your own itch. Build what you personally need; you are then the first tester and the most honest critic.
- Cut the feature list until the product is a clear, small, finished thing. A product that does one thing extremely well beats a roadmap that does everything okay.
- Ship before you are ready. A finished small thing teaches you more than an infinite plan.

## The yes/no discipline
- Every requested feature is a cost: maintenance, testing, complexity, mental load. The default answer is no unless the feature removes a real pain for a real audience.
- Ask: does this help the customer, or does it feed our ego? Features for prestige are waste.
- Ask: if we ship without this, what actually breaks? If nothing breaks, cut it.

## Audience and scope
- Decide on a small, specific audience that you can serve completely. A product that delights a niche beats one that vaguely serves everyone.
- Say no to the person who is not your customer. Attempting to please every visitor dilutes the design.

## The always-on business test
- The business should run while you sleep. If every operation needs a human hero to babysit it, it is a job, not a business. Automate the repetitive parts, document the rest, and remove the work that only existed because someone liked being needed.

## Applying to n8n/automation builds
- Before building an automation, write the smallest possible version that solves the core pain. Ship it, measure, then extend.
- Reject workflow features that do not map to a real recurring pain. A node that no queue needs is dead weight.
- Prefer a simple two-node flow that runs unattended over a twenty-node flow that needs daily care.

## Hard rules
- Never add a feature because it is easy to add. Add it because removing the pain is worth the future cost.
- Never confuse motion with progress: meetings, renames, and restructures that do not shrink queues are busywork.
- Never keep a process that only exists because someone built it once; delete it and see what breaks (usually nothing).
